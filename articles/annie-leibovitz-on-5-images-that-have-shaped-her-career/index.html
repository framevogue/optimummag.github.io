Annie Leibovitz on 5 Images That Have Shaped Her Career

June 12, 2026 - 5 min read

Annie Leibovitz is arguably the most famous living photographer. Her portraits — of presidents, rock stars, actors, and royalty — have defined the visual language of celebrity for over four decades. When she sat down with Vogue to discuss five individual pictures from her book Annie Leibovitz: Portraits 2005–2016, she offered a rare behind-the-scenes glimpse into how one of the world's greatest image-makers thinks, works, and sees.

The Power of Preparation and Spontaneity

What emerges from Leibovitz's reflections is a delicate balance between meticulous planning and openness to the unexpected. She arrives at every shoot with a clear concept — she's famous for her elaborate sets and detailed pre-production. But she also knows that the most powerful images often come from moments that no amount of planning could have anticipated. The skill, she suggests, is in creating the conditions for magic to happen and then having the presence of mind to recognize and capture it when it does.

Building Trust With Your Subject

Leibovitz's portraits are notable not just for their technical brilliance but for the emotional access they seem to grant. How does she get world leaders and Hollywood icons to let their guard down? The answer, across decades of interviews, is remarkably consistent: she invests time. She doesn't rush. She talks to her subjects, learns about them, finds common ground. She treats every portrait session as a collaboration rather than a transaction. The camera, in her hands, becomes almost incidental — a tool for recording a human connection that has already been established.

The Importance of the Print

In an era dominated by digital screens, Leibovitz remains a fierce advocate for the physical print. She has spoken extensively about how the experience of seeing a photograph as a physical object — held in your hands or hung on a wall — is fundamentally different from viewing it on a phone or monitor. The scale, the texture of the paper, the way light interacts with the surface: these material qualities are part of the photograph's meaning. For photographers who have only ever seen their work on screens, Leibovitz's insistence on printing is a powerful reminder that photography is, at its root, a physical medium.

Learning From Every Frame

One of the most striking aspects of Leibovitz's career is her willingness to evolve. She began as a photojournalist for Rolling Stone in the 1970s, chronicling the counterculture with raw, intimate images. She later transitioned into the highly produced, conceptually elaborate portrait style for which she's now best known. And she continues to push herself into new territory — landscape work, personal documentary projects, and more. The lesson for photographers at any stage of their career is clear: never stop learning, never stop experimenting, and never believe you've figured it all out.

Practical Takeaways for Photographers

While few of us will ever have the resources or access of an Annie Leibovitz production, her fundamental principles are universally applicable. Prepare thoroughly for every shoot — know your location, your lighting plan, and your concept. Build genuine rapport with your subject before you start shooting. Be open to happy accidents and unexpected moments. Print your best work and experience it as a physical object. And never, ever stop pushing yourself into unfamiliar territory.

Practical Tips

  • Plan your shoots meticulously but stay open to spontaneous moments
  • Invest time in building trust with your subject before raising the camera
  • Print your work — experiencing photography as a physical object changes everything
  • Study the masters but develop your own visual language
  • Evolve continuously — the photographers who last are the ones who never stop learning
  • Treat every shoot as a collaboration, not a transaction

Conclusion

Annie Leibovitz's career is a masterclass in growth, preparation, and human connection. The technical aspects of photography can be learned by anyone with dedication. What sets Leibovitz apart is her ability to see people — to earn their trust, understand their essence, and translate that understanding into images that feel both monumental and deeply intimate. That's not a technical skill. It's a way of being in the world.

FAQ

What camera does Annie Leibovitz use? Leibovitz has used a variety of cameras throughout her career, from 35mm film cameras in her early Rolling Stone days to medium format and large format systems. In recent years, she has also embraced digital. Her gear choices have always served her vision rather than defining it.

How does Annie Leibovitz get celebrities to pose so naturally? She invests significant time in building rapport before and during the shoot. She talks to her subjects, learns about them, and treats the session as a collaboration. The trust she establishes allows her subjects to let their guard down in ways they rarely do for other photographers.

What lighting does Annie Leibovitz use? Leibovitz's lighting setups vary enormously depending on the concept. She's known for elaborate studio lighting on her celebrity portraits but also works extensively with natural light. Her approach is always in service of the image's narrative rather than a signature lighting style.

Why does Annie Leibovitz emphasize printing photographs? She believes that photographs are meant to be physical objects. The experience of holding a print or seeing an image at scale on a wall is fundamentally different from viewing it on a screen. Printing forces photographers to engage with their work more seriously and intentionally.

How did Annie Leibovitz start her career? She began as a photojournalist for Rolling Stone magazine in the early 1970s, where her intimate, fly-on-the-wall style captured the spirit of the era's counterculture. She became the magazine's chief photographer in 1973 and later transitioned to Vanity Fair and Vogue, where her style evolved into the iconic celebrity portraiture she's known for today.
